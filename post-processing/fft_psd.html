FFT and power spectral density¶

A time signal from a simulation or a measurement, such as a wall pressure or a skin-friction coefficient at one point, usually looks irregular. A spectrum shows which frequencies the signal contains and how much each contributes.

To keep things simple, this notebook analyses a signal that we build ourselves from known ingredients. Every step can then be checked against the known answer.

1. The test signal¶

The signal is the sum of two sine waves and random noise:

$$ x(t) = A_1 \sin(2\pi f_1 t) + A_2 \sin(2\pi f_2 t) + n(t), $$

where $n(t)$ is white noise with standard deviation $\sigma_n$. The spectral analysis should recover the two frequencies $f_1$ and $f_2$ and their amplitudes $A_1$ and $A_2$.

In [1]:
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from scipy.signal import welch

# Known ingredients
A1, f1 = 1.0, 5.0      # amplitude, frequency [Hz]
A2, f2 = 0.5, 12.0
SIGMA_N = 1.0          # standard deviation of the noise

# Sampling
FS = 100.0             # sampling frequency [Hz]
T  = 60.0              # record length [s]

N = int(FS*T)
t = np.arange(N)/FS
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
x = A1*np.sin(2*np.pi*f1*t) + A2*np.sin(2*np.pi*f2*t) + SIGMA_N*rng.standard_normal(N)

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(9, 3))
show = t <= 2.0
ax.plot(t[show], x[show], color='0.3', lw=1, label='signal $x(t)$')
ax.plot(t[show], (A1*np.sin(2*np.pi*f1*t) + A2*np.sin(2*np.pi*f2*t))[show],
        color='tab:blue', lw=1.5, label='sine waves only')
ax.set_xlabel('$t$ [s]'); ax.set_ylabel('$x$')
ax.set_title('First 2 s of the signal')
ax.legend(loc='upper right', fontsize=8); ax.grid(alpha=0.3)
plt.show()

With the noise added, the two sine waves are hard to see in the time signal. The spectrum will separate them.

2. Sampling¶

A sampled signal consists of $N$ values taken at a fixed interval $\Delta t = 1/f_s$, where $f_s$ is the sampling frequency, over a record of length $T = N\,\Delta t$. Two numbers follow from this and limit every spectrum:

  • Nyquist frequency $f_\text{Ny} = f_s/2$: the highest frequency that can be represented.
  • Frequency resolution $\Delta f = 1/T = f_s/N$: the spacing between neighbouring frequencies in the spectrum.

The resolution depends only on the record length: sampling faster raises $f_\text{Ny}$, but only a longer record gives a finer $\Delta f$.

Aliasing. A frequency above $f_\text{Ny}$ is not lost but shows up at a wrong, lower frequency. In the example below, a 12 Hz sine sampled at 15 Hz ($f_\text{Ny} = 7.5$ Hz) is indistinguishable from a 3 Hz sine. Aliasing cannot be undone after sampling. In a simulation, $f_s$ is set by how often the data is written out, not by the solver time step.

print(f"N = {N},  T = {T:g} s,  f_s = {FS:g} Hz")
print(f"Nyquist frequency     f_Ny = {FS/2:g} Hz")
print(f"Frequency resolution  Δf   = {1/T:.4f} Hz")

3. FFT and amplitude spectrum¶

The discrete Fourier transform writes the signal as a sum of sine waves at the frequencies $f_k = k\,\Delta f$:

$$ X_k = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x_n\, e^{-2\pi i\, k n/N}, \qquad k = 0, 1, \dots, N/2 . $$

The FFT (fast Fourier transform) is simply a fast algorithm for computing it. For a real signal, the frequencies above $f_\text{Ny}$ mirror those below, so only $k = 0 \dots N/2$ is kept (one-sided spectrum). The amplitude of the sine wave at $f_k$ is then

$$ \hat A_k = \frac{2\,|X_k|}{N}. $$

In [4]:
X = np.fft.rfft(x)
f = np.fft.rfftfreq(N, d=1/FS)
amp = 2*np.abs(X)/N

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(9, 3))
ax.plot(f, amp, color='tab:blue', lw=0.8)
ax.set_xlabel('$f$ [Hz]'); ax.set_ylabel(r'$\hat A$')
ax.set_title('Amplitude spectrum')
ax.set_xlim(0, FS/2); ax.grid(alpha=0.3)
for fk, Ak in ((f1, A1), (f2, A2)):
    k = np.argmin(np.abs(f - fk))
    ax.annotate(f'{f[k]:g} Hz, $\\hat A$ = {amp[k]:.2f}', xy=(f[k], amp[k]), xytext=(f[k] + 2, amp[k]),
                fontsize=9, va='center')
plt.show()

The two peaks sit at $f_1$ and $f_2$ with heights close to $A_1$ and $A_2$. The small remaining differences come from the noise. The noise itself is spread evenly over all frequencies, forming a low floor.

Spectral leakage. Here $f_1$ and $f_2$ fall exactly on frequencies $f_k$ of the spectrum. A real signal rarely does, and its energy then leaks into neighbouring frequencies, lowering and widening the peak. Multiplying the signal by a window function that tapers smoothly to zero at both ends, usually the Hann window, reduces leakage. It is used in Welch's method below.

4. Power spectral density¶

For signals with broadband content, such as turbulence or noise, amplitudes of individual sine waves are not meaningful. Instead, the power spectral density (PSD) $S(f)$ describes how the signal's energy, i.e. its variance, is distributed over frequency. The one-sided PSD computed from one FFT of the whole record is called the periodogram:

$$ S(f_k) = \frac{2\,|X_k|^2}{f_s N} \qquad (\text{without the factor } 2 \text{ at } k = 0 \text{ and } k = N/2). $$

Its units are (signal unit)² per Hz. Integrating it over frequency gives back the variance of the signal, which is Parseval's theorem:

$$ \int_0^{f_\text{Ny}} S(f)\, \mathrm df = \sigma_x^2 . $$

Welch's method¶

The periodogram is very noisy: each value comes from a single FFT, and a longer record only makes the spectrum finer, not smoother. Welch's method reduces the scatter by averaging:

  1. Split the signal into segments of length $L$, usually overlapping by 50 %.
  2. Multiply each segment by a window (Hann) and compute its periodogram.
  3. Average the periodograms.

The price is a coarser resolution, $\Delta f = f_s/L$. Choosing $L$ is therefore a trade-off: short segments give a smooth spectrum with coarse resolution, long segments give fine resolution but more scatter.

In [5]:
S_per = 2*np.abs(X)**2/(FS*N)
S_per[0] /= 2
S_per[-1] /= 2

SEG = 1000                                    # segment length L (10 s)
f_w, S_w = welch(x, fs=FS, window='hann', nperseg=SEG, noverlap=SEG//2)

print(f"Expected variance            : {A1**2/2 + A2**2/2 + SIGMA_N**2:.4f}   (A1²/2 + A2²/2 + σn²)")
print(f"Variance of the signal       : {np.var(x):.4f}")
print(f"Area under the periodogram   : {np.trapezoid(S_per, f):.4f}")
print(f"Area under the Welch PSD     : {np.trapezoid(S_w, f_w):.4f}")
print(f"Welch: {N//(SEG//2) - 1} segments,  Δf = {FS/SEG:g} Hz")

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(9, 3.5))
ax.loglog(f[1:], S_per[1:], color='0.7', lw=0.6, label='periodogram (one FFT)')
ax.loglog(f_w[1:], S_w[1:], color='tab:blue', lw=1.5, label=f'Welch ($L$ = {SEG}, Hann, 50 % overlap)')
ax.axhline(2*SIGMA_N**2/FS, color='k', ls=':', lw=1, label=r'white-noise level $2\sigma_n^2/f_s$')
ax.set_xlabel('$f$ [Hz]'); ax.set_ylabel('$S(f)$')
ax.set_title('Power spectral density')
ax.set_xlim(f[1], FS/2)
ax.legend(fontsize=8, loc='lower left'); ax.grid(alpha=0.3, which='both')
plt.show()

Expected variance            : 1.6250   (A1²/2 + A2²/2 + σn²)
Variance of the signal       : 1.6064
Area under the periodogram   : 1.6064
Area under the Welch PSD     : 1.5984
Welch: 11 segments,  Δf = 0.1 Hz

The area under the periodogram equals the variance exactly, as Parseval's theorem requires. The Welch estimate agrees to within about half a percent; the windowing and averaging cause small deviations. Both are close to the expected variance: each sine wave contributes $A^2/2$, and the noise contributes $\sigma_n^2$.

Welch's method gives a smooth spectrum: a flat noise floor at the white-noise level, and two clear peaks.

The height of a sine-wave peak in a PSD depends on $\Delta f$, because the whole power of the sine wave, $A^2/2$, falls into one frequency bin of width $\Delta f$. For a pure tone, the area under the peak is meaningful, not its height.

5. Premultiplied spectrum and dominant frequency¶

Spectra are usually plotted on logarithmic axes. There, the area under $S(f)$ no longer shows the energy content, which makes it hard to see which frequencies dominate. Since $\mathrm df = f\,\mathrm d(\ln f)$, Parseval's theorem can be rewritten as

$$ \sigma_x^2 = \int S(f)\,\mathrm df = \int f\,S(f)\; \mathrm d(\ln f). $$

In a plot of the premultiplied spectrum $f\,S(f)$ over a logarithmic frequency axis, equal areas therefore mean equal energy. Dividing by the variance, $E(f) = S(f)/\sigma_x^2$, normalises the total area to 1. Signals with different amplitudes can then be compared directly. The dominant frequency is the peak of $f\,E(f)$.

In [6]:
E = S_w/np.trapezoid(S_w, f_w)          # normalised to unit area (unit variance)
fE = f_w*E
k_pk = np.argmax(fE[1:]) + 1
f_pk = f_w[k_pk]

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(9, 3.5))
ax.loglog(f_w[1:], fE[1:], color='tab:blue', lw=1.5)
ax.axvline(f_pk, color='tab:red', ls='--', lw=1, label=f'dominant frequency = {f_pk:g} Hz')
ax.set_xlabel('$f$ [Hz]'); ax.set_ylabel(r'$f\,E(f)$')
ax.set_title('Premultiplied, normalised spectrum')
ax.set_xlim(f_w[1], FS/2)
ax.legend(fontsize=9, loc='lower right'); ax.grid(alpha=0.3, which='both')
plt.show()

The dominant frequency is $f_1 = 5$ Hz, the stronger of the two sine waves. Its precision is limited by the resolution of the Welch spectrum, $\Delta f = f_s/L$.

Note that at high frequencies the white noise rises in the premultiplied plot, because $f\,S(f)$ grows with $f$ for a flat $S(f)$. A broadband signal can therefore look as if it dominates at high frequencies in this representation, and the peak should be checked against the plain PSD.
